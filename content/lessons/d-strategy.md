# Data Insights Section Strategy

Data Insights (DI) is the section most people under-prepare, and it's also the one where strategy moves your score the most. You get 45 minutes for 20 questions, which is 2 minutes and 15 seconds each, with five very different question types in one mixed bag. The content isn't the hard part; the clock is.

## The shape of the section

| Type | Typical count | What it tests | Target time |
|---|---|---|---|
| Data Sufficiency (DS) | 5 to 7 | Is the information enough? | 1.5 to 2.5 min |
| Multi-Source Reasoning (MSR) | 6 to 9 (2 to 3 sets) | Combining tabs, rules, inferences | 2 to 3 min each (7 to 8 per set) |
| Table Analysis (TA) | 2 to 4 | Sorting and reading a table, three statements | 2 to 3 min |
| Graphics Interpretation (GI) | 2 to 4 | Reading a chart, completing two statements | 1.5 to 2.5 min |
| Two-Part Analysis (TPA) | 2 to 4 | Two linked answers | 2 to 3 min |

The mix changes from one exam to the next. Don't count on a specific split. What you can count on is that the on-screen calculator is available and that no question needs advanced maths.

> [!REMEMBER]
> 45 minutes ÷ 20 questions = 2 min 15 sec on average. Your job is not to spend the average on every question. It's to bank time on the quick ones so you can afford the slow ones.

## Pacing: where the time comes from

Think in checkpoints. These are the places where you glance at the clock and decide whether you're ahead or behind.

| After question | Time used (target) | Time left (target) |
|---|---|---|
| 5 | 11 min | 34 min |
| 10 | 22 min | 23 min |
| 15 | 33 min | 12 min |
| 20 | 43 min | 2 min (for review) |

If you're behind by more than two minutes at a checkpoint, speed up on the next few questions by choosing which types to rush (below) rather than by rushing everything.

### Types to speed through

- **Graphics Interpretation (GI).** Often the fastest: read the chart, read the sentence, fill the blanks. Aim for 90 seconds to 2 minutes. Slow down only when a blank needs a calculation.
- **Table Analysis (TA).** If you sort the right column first, each statement takes 20 to 30 seconds. Sort once, answer three times.
- **Data Sufficiency with a clear first statement.** If statement 1 alone obviously answers it, check statement 2 alone and finish. Don't over-verify.
- **Easy MSR questions.** The first question in a set is often a simple lookup. Don't linger.

### Types to slow down on

- **Hard DS** (tricky Yes/No questions, number properties). Rushing here costs points because the traps are built for speed.
- **MSR's first question.** You pay the skimming cost there, so it takes longer and that's fine.
- **Quant TPA.** A single misread equation sinks both columns.

## The "abandon one" strategy

Every DI section contains at least one question that will cost you four or five minutes, whatever you do. The smartest plan is to decide, before the exam, that you will deliberately give up on one or two questions and guess on them. Here's how:

1. Set a mental trigger: if you're 90 seconds in and don't have a plan (not an answer, a plan), you've hit a time sink.
2. Make a smart guess right away. Eliminate what you can, choose one answer, and bookmark the question.
3. Move on without regret. A wrong answer on one question costs far less than the two or three questions you can't finish at the end.

> [!TIP]
> Guessing early beats guessing late. Rushed, panicked guesses in the last 30 seconds are worse than a calm guess at minute 12. Also, unanswered questions are penalised, so never leave anything blank.

> [!TRAP]
> The sunk-cost trap: "I've already spent three minutes, I can't stop now." Those three minutes are gone either way. The only question is whether the next minute is better spent here or on the next question.

## Calculator discipline

The on-screen calculator is a speed tool, not a thinking tool.

- Use it for multi-digit multiplication, division, and percentages with ugly numbers.
- Don't use it for 15% of 200, or 3 × 8. The click-and-read time is slower than your head.
- Write every intermediate result on your noteboard. Typing the same long multiplication twice is the classic way to lose 40 seconds.
- Check order and parentheses. The on-screen calculator is a simple one; work out brackets yourself.
- Sanity-check magnitudes. If an answer is 100 times bigger than your estimate, you've mistyped.

## Guessing on multi-part items

TPA, TA, and MSR yes/no questions need several correct selections. That changes your guessing maths: if you're unsure about even one part, the whole question is likely lost. So:

- On a three-statement question, spend your time on the statement you're least sure of, not the ones you've already settled.
- Use the structure: in many TPAs, once you've fixed column 1, column 2 follows from the linking condition. Your confidence in one part can rescue the other.
- If you truly can't resolve one part, answer the rest with your best reasoning. A partly informed guess beats a random one.
- Never leave a column or statement blank. A question with a blank part is simply wrong.

## Using Review & Edit

At the end of each section, GMAT Focus lets you revisit questions you bookmarked and change up to three answers. It's a useful safety net, but only if you've set it up as you go.

- **Bookmark while you work.** When you guess or feel unsure, click the bookmark and move on. That costs two seconds.
- **Reserve 2 to 3 minutes.** Plan to finish the 20th question with about 3 minutes left. Review & Edit is a tool for the end, not a place to spend your main time.
- **Pick the most fixable questions.** With three changes, choose items where a fresh look might help: a calculation you rushed, a bookmark with an obvious error check. Don't reopen a question you abandoned because it was a time sink.
- **Change an answer only for a reason.** If you can state why the new answer is better, change it. If you only feel nervous, leave it.

> [!NOTE]
> Review & Edit changes your answers but doesn't re-adapt the questions you've already seen. It's a corrections window, not a second attempt at the whole test.

## Where to put Data Insights in your exam

You can take the three sections in any order. There's no right answer, but there are patterns.

| Order | Why people choose it | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| DI first | You're freshest for the section that rewards focus and fast switching. | A rocky start can dent confidence for Quant and Verbal. |
| Quant, Verbal, DI | Starts with your strongest section, builds confidence. DI last benefits from warm-up on numbers. | Fatigue: DI is mentally busy. Take the break before it if you can. |
| Verbal, Quant, DI | Common if Verbal is your strongest and you want an early win. | Same fatigue risk at the end. |

Practical advice for Maria: decide your order from mock exams, not from guesses. Take two full mocks in two different orders and compare section scores. Then use the 10-minute break wisely: eat something, move, and don't think about the last section.

## A weekly DI drill plan

You'll mostly study on your phone in short sessions, with longer weekend blocks. This plan uses both.

| Day | Session | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Mon | 20 min | 6 DS questions untimed, then review traps. Focus on one DS trap type. |
| Tue | 20 min | 4 TPA questions, 2.5 minutes each. Write the linking condition before solving. |
| Wed | 15 min | Data literacy: 3 TA or GI items. Practise sorting and estimating. |
| Thu | 20 min | One MSR set (3 questions). Skim tabs, write one line per tab, then answer. |
| Fri | Rest or 10 min | Review your error log. |
| Sat | 60 to 75 min | Timed mini-section: 10 mixed DI questions in 22 minutes, then 30 minutes of review. |
| Sun | 30 min | Redo every question you missed that week. Tag each miss as concept, careless, or time. |

Keep a simple error log: question type, what went wrong, and the fix. After two weeks, the pattern of your errors will tell you what to drill.

## Strategy in action

### Worked example 1 (Easy)

At the 10-question checkpoint your clock shows 25 minutes used. What should you do?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

You're 3 minutes behind the 22-minute target. Don't rush everything. Choose to move faster on the next GI, TA, and clear-cut DS questions (aim for 1.5 to 2 minutes), and be ready to abandon one time sink. If you run out of time later, you'd rather have guessed deliberately than skipped.

Trap: slowing down to "be careful" when behind. That compounds the problem.

</details>

### Worked example 2 (Medium)

You're 100 seconds into a Data Sufficiency problem about integers and you have no idea how to start. The clock is on track. What now?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

Try one rescue move in the next 20 seconds: test two small numbers or restate the question as an equation. If nothing clicks, guess (for DS, "E" or "C" are tempting but not always right; eliminate a statement that is clearly insufficient first), bookmark it, and move on. You've protected the time for the next questions.

Trap: staying for "just one more minute". That minute becomes four.

</details>

### Worked example 3 (Medium)

A Table Analysis question has three statements about a table of 12 cities. You haven't sorted the table. Is that a good use of time?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

Sort first. Pick the column most statements refer to and sort once. Statements about "the highest" or "the median" become a glance. Sorting costs 5 seconds and saves a minute.

Trap: scanning visually for the maximum and accidentally missing a row.

</details>

### Worked example 4 (Hard)

You've finished question 20 with 3 minutes left, and you've bookmarked 5 questions. You may change up to three answers. Which do you reopen?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

Rank the bookmarks: reopen first the ones where you made a quick calculation and can re-check it in 30 seconds, then ones where you eliminated three options and were torn between two. Leave the time sinks you deliberately guessed on; a minute of new thinking rarely solves what four minutes couldn't.

Trap: changing answers just because you feel anxious. Change only when you can state a concrete reason.

</details>

## Quick check

1. What's the average time per DI question, and what should your checkpoint be after 10 questions?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

</details>

2. Why guess early rather than late?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

An early guess is calm and informed (you can still eliminate options), and it frees time for questions you can actually win. Late guesses are rushed.

</details>

3. How many answers can you change in Review & Edit?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Up to three per section.

</details>

4. Name one type to speed through and one to slow down on.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>

Speed through GI or sorted TA statements. Slow down on hard DS or Quant TPA, where small misreads sink the whole question.

</details>

## Key takeaways

- 20 questions in 45 minutes is about 2 min 15 sec each. Checkpoints at 5, 10 and 15 questions keep you honest.
- Bank time on GI, sorted TA, and clean DS so you can afford MSR and hard DS.
- Plan to abandon one or two time sinks. Guess calmly, bookmark, and move on.
- Use the calculator for ugly arithmetic only. Write intermediate results on your noteboard.
- Never leave any part of a multi-part question blank.
- Bookmark as you go, and keep 2 to 3 minutes for Review & Edit. Change answers only for concrete reasons.
- Decide your section order from mock results, and follow a weekly plan with an error log.
