# Mindset, Energy and Staying the Course

The GMAT tests your reasoning, but your preparation tests something else: whether you can keep going for ten months while work, family and life carry on. This lesson is about the part nobody puts in a syllabus. It covers how to study as a working professional, manage your energy, handle nerves, and recover from the bad days.

## Studying with a full-time job

You have limited time and limited energy, and the second one is usually the real constraint. A few principles help.

**Protect your slots.** A study session that exists only in your head loses to every meeting and every message. Put your sessions in your calendar with a clear name, like "GMAT: ratios set". Treat them as client commitments. You can move one, but you shouldn't delete it without moving it somewhere.

**Match the task to the energy.** You don't have the same brain at 7 a.m. and 10 p.m.

| Your energy | Good task |
|---|---|
| Sharp (usually morning or weekend) | New topics, hard questions, timed sets, mocks |
| Medium | Practice sets, error-log review |
| Low (late evening, after a hard day) | Flashcards, re-reading an explanation, light review |

If you only have low-energy evenings on weekdays, that's fine. Put the heavy work on Saturday morning and let the weekdays be gentle and regular.

**Use the small gaps.** Ten minutes on the Delhi Metro is enough for a flashcard set or three practice questions. You won't do your best learning there, but you'll keep the habit warm, and those minutes add up over the weeks.

**Set a "minimum day".** Decide in advance what counts as a bare-minimum day: for example, one flashcard session and one question. On terrible days you do the minimum and stop without guilt. That keeps your streak alive, and streaks matter more than perfect days.

**Be honest with your team.** You don't need to announce your plan, but if you have a heavy week at work, adjust in advance, not afterwards. If you know a campaign launches in the second week of March, plan a lighter study week then.

> [!TIP]
> Keep a "low-effort list" in the app: five things you can do in 10 minutes when you have no energy. When you're tired, you shouldn't have to decide anything.

## Energy management

Sleep, food and movement aren't extras. They decide whether your study hours count.

**Sleep.** Memory settles during sleep, and a tired brain makes careless errors. Aim for a regular sleep time most nights, around 7 to 8 hours. Don't trade sleep for study. An extra hour of tired studying is usually worth less than an hour of rest. Be especially careful in the week before your exam.

**Food and water.** Studying on an empty stomach or after a heavy meal both hurt focus. Eat regularly, keep water nearby, and notice how you feel after different meals. Use the same pattern you plan for exam day, so there are no surprises.

**Movement.** A short walk before or after studying helps many people think more clearly. You don't need a gym plan. Twenty minutes of walking counts.

**Breaks.** Work in focused blocks of 25 to 45 minutes, then take 5 to 10 minutes away from the screen. Looking at your phone doesn't count as a break from a phone-based app.

**One full rest day a week.** Not a "light study" day. A real day off. This is part of the plan, and it protects you from burnout.

## Anxiety and test-day nerves

Some nerves before an exam are normal and even useful. They sharpen your attention. The problem starts when anxiety takes over your thinking. Here are tools that work, and you should practise them before you need them.

### Slow breathing

When you're anxious, your breathing speeds up, which makes you feel worse. Slow breathing sends the opposite signal. Try this: breathe in through your nose for 4 seconds, then breathe out slowly through your mouth for 6 to 8 seconds. Repeat five times. The longer exhale is the key part. Practise it in your daily life, in the Metro or at your desk, so it feels natural when you need it.

### Reframing

How you describe the feeling changes how it affects you. Research on exam anxiety has found that telling yourself "I'm excited" or "my body is getting ready" can help people perform better than trying to force calm. The effect isn't huge, and it won't suit everyone, but it costs nothing to try.

Some reframes to practise:

- "I'm nervous" becomes "My body is getting ready to perform."
- "I have to score 700" becomes "My job is to answer each question as well as I can."
- "This question is impossible" becomes "This question is a hard one, and I know what to do: guess smartly and move on."
- "I'm failing" becomes "A hard streak can mean I'm doing well."

### Focus on the next question

Anxiety lives in the future ("What if I miss my target?") or in the past ("I got that one wrong"). The only question you can affect is the one on the screen. After each answer, take one breath and reset.

### Rehearse the day

Familiarity lowers fear. Take mocks in realistic conditions. Visit your test centre beforehand if you can. Do the journey once at the same time of day. Know the check-in process. The more of the day you've already lived through, the less is left to scare you.

> [!NOTE]
> If anxiety feels heavy, constant, or is affecting your sleep and daily life, not just your exam, please talk to a doctor or a counsellor. That's not a GMAT problem; that's a health question, and it deserves proper support.

## Sleep before the exam

The night before is less important than the week before. One bad night won't ruin you if the previous few nights were decent. Still, give yourself the best chance:

- Stop studying the evening before. A light flashcard skim is fine, but no new topics.
- Prepare your bag, documents and clothes in advance.
- Keep your usual bedtime. Don't try to sleep far earlier than normal, as that often backfires.
- Avoid heavy meals, alcohol and late-night screens.
- If you can't sleep, don't panic. Rest still helps, and your adrenaline will get you through.

## Burnout: signs and what to do

Burnout sneaks up. It isn't just tiredness. Watch for these signs:

- Dreading every session, even short ones.
- Making careless errors you normally wouldn't.
- Scores dropping across several sessions, not just one.
- Feeling irritable, flat or hopeless about the goal.
- Sleeping badly, or sleeping a lot and still feeling tired.
- Skipping sessions and feeling guilty rather than relieved.

If you notice several, here's what to do:

1. **Take two or three full days off.** Genuinely off. The plan can absorb it.
2. **Lighten the load** for a week: flashcards, light practice, no timed work.
3. **Check the basics:** sleep, food, movement, and the amount you're working.
4. **Reconnect with your reason.** Read the sentence you wrote in week one.
5. **Talk to someone.** A friend or family member, or a mentor who has done this.
6. **Reset the plan, not the goal.** If you're consistently overloaded, ask whether 8 to 10 hours a week is realistic, and adjust the schedule.

> [!REMEMBER]
> A planned week off costs you a little progress. Burnout can cost you months. Rest is cheaper.

## Motivation dips

Motivation isn't constant. Around months three to five, almost everyone hits a flat stretch: the novelty is gone, the exam is still far away, and progress feels slow. That's not a sign you're doing it wrong. It's the middle.

What helps:

- **Rely on routine, not mood.** If you only study when you feel like it, you'll skip many days. Habits don't need motivation. They need a trigger, such as "after dinner, I open the app".
- **Make progress visible.** Open the Progress screen weekly. Look at accuracy in a topic from two months ago. Seeing real improvement is the best fuel.
- **Break the goal into pieces.** "Score 680" is far away. "Finish the percents lesson and get 7 of 10" is today.
- **Celebrate small wins.** Finished a phase? Hit a streak? Do something nice.
- **Study with someone** when possible, even just a friend who is also preparing for something. A weekly check-in works.
- **Revisit your reason.** Picture the programme you want, and what it opens up.
- **Change the format.** If you're tired of questions, spend a session on the lessons, or on reading the Economist for your Verbal habit.

## What to do after a bad mock

Sooner or later, a mock will come back lower than you hoped. Here's how to handle it.

1. **Give yourself 30 minutes of feeling bad.** That's allowed. Then stop.
2. **Remember the noise.** Scores wobble by dozens of points from day to day. One mock is a data point, not a prediction.
3. **Check the conditions.** Were you tired, rushed, hungry or interrupted?
4. **Look at what's fixable.** How many errors were careless, misread or timing? Those are the quickest to improve.
5. **Do the full review.** It's more useful to study a bad mock than a good one.
6. **Pick three actions** and put them in your plan.
7. **Don't change everything.** Resist the urge to switch courses, plans or resources after one disappointing result.
8. **Talk to someone.** Not about the score, but about how you're feeling.

A phrase worth keeping: "That mock showed me where the points are." It's more accurate than "I failed", and it points forward.

## Self-talk that helps

Your inner voice matters. Compare these.

| Unhelpful | Better |
|---|---|
| "I'm just bad at maths." | "I'm out of practice, and I'm getting back in." |
| "I'll never reach 650." | "I'm not there yet." |
| "Everyone else is better prepared." | "I'm only competing with my last mock." |
| "I wasted this week." | "I can restart tomorrow with a small session." |

None of this is about being falsely cheerful. It's about speaking to yourself the way a good friend would.

## Key takeaways

- Put study slots in your calendar and match hard tasks to high-energy times.
- Set a "minimum day" so a bad day never breaks the habit.
- Sleep, food, movement and a weekly rest day are part of the study plan.
- Practise slow breathing (a longer exhale) and reframe nerves as readiness.
- Rehearse exam conditions so test day holds fewer surprises.
- Know the signs of burnout and respond early with rest and a lighter week.
- Expect a motivation dip in the middle months, and lean on routine, visible progress and your reason.
- After a bad mock, review it fully, pick three actions, and keep the plan.
- If anxiety or low mood is heavy or constant, talk to a doctor or counsellor.
