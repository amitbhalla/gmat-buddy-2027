# Offers, Waitlists and Plan B

The emails will arrive in waves, and not all of them will be the one you wanted. Whatever lands in your inbox, you'll have choices. This lesson covers how to handle waitlists, compare offers, negotiate scholarships, and what to do if the cycle doesn't go to plan.

> [!NOTE]
> Policies on waitlists, deposits and scholarship reviews differ by school. What follows is general practice. Read each school's own rules, and when in doubt, ask admissions in a polite email.

## The four possible outcomes

| Outcome | What it means | Your next step |
|---|---|---|
| Admit | You're in, with or without a scholarship | Compare, decide, deposit |
| Waitlist | Not yet; maybe later | Follow the school's instructions, update them |
| Reject | Not this time | Ask for feedback if offered, plan next steps |
| Deferred or "pending" | Moved to a later round | Wait, update if allowed |

## If you're waitlisted

A waitlist isn't a no. It's a "we like you and don't have a seat yet". Typical steps:

1. **Read the instructions.** Some schools say don't send anything; others welcome updates. Follow the rule exactly.
2. **Confirm your interest.** If the school asks, say clearly that you'd accept an offer.
3. **Send one strong update** (if allowed), not many small ones. Good updates: a new GMAT score, a promotion or a major result, a new course, a new leadership role.
4. **Keep your options alive.** Don't stop other applications, and put down a deposit elsewhere if you must. Deposits are usually non-refundable, so read the terms.
5. **Don't badger.** Weekly emails don't help.

> [!TIP]
> An update is useful only if it's new information. A short note of 150 to 200 words is plenty: what changed, how it connects to your goals, and that this school remains your first choice (only say it if it's true).

LBS MiM uses a video interview only for waitlisted candidates, according to its page. If you're waitlisted there, practise the video format.

## Choosing between offers

Sit down with your tracker and compare programmes on the same dimensions.

| Dimension | Questions |
|---|---|
| Career outcomes | Which programme places people in the roles I want, in the city I want? |
| Cost and funding | What's the total cost after scholarships? What's the loan and repayment? |
| Fit | Did I enjoy talking to students? Would I want to live there? |
| Network | Who will I meet, and where do alumni work? |
| Life | Visa, language, climate, family and friends nearby |
| Risk | If my plan changes, how flexible is this degree? |

### A simple scoring method

Give each dimension a weight out of 100 that reflects your priorities, score each school from 1 to 5, and multiply. Don't treat the score as an answer. Use it to notice when your gut disagrees with your spreadsheet. If it does, ask why.

> [!TRAP]
> Don't choose purely on brand. Prestige helps, but a programme that fits your goals and fits your finances usually serves you better than a famous name with a heavy loan.

### Talk to people

Call or message two current students and one alumna from each programme. Ask: what do you wish you'd known? What's the culture like? What did you do about funding? Women in the class, in particular, can tell you what the day-to-day is like.

## Negotiating scholarships

Many schools allow you to ask for a review of a scholarship, especially if you hold a better offer elsewhere. Handle it professionally.

1. **Check whether the school allows it.** Some say their awards are final.
2. **Be factual and polite.** Say you're excited about the school, name the competing offer and its value, and ask whether they could reconsider.
3. **Attach evidence** (a copy of the offer letter), if the school asks for it.
4. **Give a reason beyond money.** For instance, what you'd contribute to the class.
5. **Accept the answer.** Don't threaten, and don't bluff.

ISB's FAQ says applicants are automatically considered for merit-based scholarships and that waivers range from 25% to 100%. Ask ISB admissions directly what the process is if you wish to discuss your award.

> [!REMEMBER]
> Never lie about a competing offer. Admissions offices talk to each other, and trust is the one thing you can't repair.

## Deposits and deadlines

- Read the acceptance deadline. Don't let it slip.
- Understand what the deposit covers and whether it's refundable.
- If you're waiting on another school, ask whether you can have an extension. Many give a short one.
- Confirm your acceptance in writing, and keep a copy.

Once you've decided, tell the other schools you're withdrawing. It frees a seat for someone on a waitlist, and it's good manners.

## Before the programme starts

The months between acceptance and day one are an opportunity. Use them well.

| Area | What to do |
|---|---|
| Quant refresh | Review algebra, statistics and basic accounting. Your GMAT work already helps |
| Finance basics | Read an introductory book or take a short online course |
| Reading | Business news, case studies, your future industry |
| Network | Connect with classmates and alumni; join the class group |
| Admin | Visa, housing, insurance, loan disbursement |
| Wellbeing | Rest, time with family; you won't have much spare time soon |
| Work | Hand over responsibilities gracefully; leave on good terms |

## If things don't go to plan

It happens to strong candidates every year. The cycle is competitive and often hinges on factors you can't see. If you don't get an offer you wanted:

### Step 1: Pause, then debrief

Give yourself a week. Then review: which parts were weakest? GMAT, story, recommendations, interviews, experience level? Ask the school for feedback if it offers it. Some do.

### Step 2: Decide your route

| Route | When it makes sense |
|---|---|
| Reapply next cycle | You have clear weaknesses you can fix and an improved profile to show |
| Apply to rolling or later-round programmes | Some schools still have places in later rounds |
| Try a different type of programme | A MiM didn't work, so try a marketing Masters or an Indian programme |
| Wait for an experienced MBA | You'll have 4 to 5 years by about 2030-2031 |

### Reapplying

ISB, for example, has a compulsory extra essay for reapplicants and a 50% waiver on the application fee, according to third-party and official sources. In your reapplication, show what changed: a new role, new results, a better score, or a clearer plan. A reapplication that repeats the first one with new wording rarely succeeds.

Also remember: your GMAT score is valid for five years. If your score is already strong, you can keep it and put your effort into the rest of your profile.

> [!NOTE]
> Time is also a plus here. Each extra year adds experience. Some programmes that were out of reach (ISB with 24 months, IIM one-year programmes with 4 to 5 years) become more realistic. A reapplication isn't a step back; it can be a step forward.

### Look after yourself

A rejection isn't a verdict on your worth. Talk to friends and family. Put your energy into the work that brings you pride. You'll be a better candidate in the next round, and a stronger person either way.

## Worked example: two offers, one waitlist

Maria receives: an ISB offer with a 25% waiver, an Imperial MSc Strategic Marketing offer with no scholarship, and a waitlist spot at LBS MiM.

<details><summary>Show how she might decide</summary>

1. **Check deadlines.** ISB and Imperial both have deposit dates. She notes them and asks for an extension if needed.
2. **Compute cost.** ISB's net cost after the waiver is lower. Imperial costs GBP 48,000 in tuition plus living.
3. **Compare outcomes.** She talks to two marketing alumni from each programme and reads the employment pages.
4. **Handle the waitlist.** She emails LBS to confirm interest only if it's still her first choice, and sends one update if she has one.
5. **Negotiate.** She asks Imperial politely whether a scholarship review is possible, citing the ISB waiver and her goals.
6. **Decide.** If she wants to work in India, ISB is the likely choice. If Europe, Imperial, funded by a loan, might win, but only if the numbers work.

Trap: waiting for the waitlist past the deposit deadline without a plan. Secure a seat you'd be happy with first.

## Key takeaways
- A waitlist is a "not yet". Follow instructions, send one strong update if allowed, and keep other options open.
- Compare offers on outcomes, cost, fit, network, life and risk, not brand alone.
- Scholarship reviews are possible at some schools; be honest, factual and polite.
- Read deposit terms and acceptance deadlines carefully.
- Use the months before the programme to refresh quant, learn finance basics and build your network.
- If it doesn't work out, debrief, fix what's fixable and reapply or switch route; your GMAT lasts five years.
