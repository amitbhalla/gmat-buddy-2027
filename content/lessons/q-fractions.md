# Fractions & Decimals

Fractions hide inside almost every GMAT Quant question: percents, ratios, rates, probability, and "what part of the work" problems are all fractions wearing different clothes. If you can handle fractions comfortably, roughly a third of the section gets easier.

The good news is that there are only a few moves to learn. Most mistakes come from rushing, not from the ideas being hard.

## What a fraction really is

A fraction a/b means "a parts, each one b-th of a whole". The top (numerator) counts the parts you have; the bottom (denominator) says how many equal parts the whole was cut into. And a/b is also just a ÷ b.

- Proper fraction: numerator smaller than denominator (3/4). Less than 1.
- Improper fraction: numerator bigger (11/4). More than 1.
- Mixed number: 2¾ means 2 + ¾. To convert to improper: 2¾ = (2 × 4 + 3)/4 = 11/4. To go back, divide: 11 ÷ 4 = 2 remainder 3, so 2¾.

**Equivalent fractions** look different but have the same value: 2/3 = 4/6 = 20/30. Multiply or divide top and bottom by the same non-zero number. Simplifying (reducing) means dividing out common factors: 18/24 = 3/4 (divide by 6).

> [!TIP]
> To reduce fast, test the divisibility rules on top and bottom: both even? halve. Digit sums both divisible by 3? Divide by 3. Both end in 0 or 5? Divide by 5.

## Adding and subtracting

You can only add or subtract pieces of the same size, so first find a **common denominator**, ideally the smallest one (the least common multiple of the denominators).

1/6 + 3/4: the LCM of 6 and 4 is 12. So 2/12 + 9/12 = 11/12.

Then add or subtract the tops and keep the bottom. Never add the denominators: 1/2 + 1/3 is 5/6, not 2/5.

> [!TRAP]
> 1/2 + 1/3 = 2/5 is the classic wrong move. Sanity check: ½ plus something positive must be more than ½, and 2/5 is less than ½.

Mixed numbers: add whole parts and fraction parts separately. 3⅔ − 1¾ = (3 − 1) + (8/12 − 9/12) = 2 − 1/12 = 1 11/12.

## Multiplying and dividing

**Multiply:** top × top, bottom × bottom, and **cancel before you multiply**. (4/9) × (15/16): 4 and 16 share 4, and 9 and 15 share 3, so you get (1/3) × (5/4) = 5/12.

"Of" means multiply: ⅔ of 18 is ⅔ × 18 = 12.

**Divide:** flip the second fraction (the reciprocal) and multiply. (3/5) ÷ (9/10) = (3/5) × (10/9) = 30/45 = 2/3.

The **reciprocal** of a/b is b/a. A number times its reciprocal is 1. The reciprocal of 5 is 1/5; of 0.25 (¼) is 4. Zero has no reciprocal.

> [!REMEMBER]
> Dividing by a fraction less than 1 makes the answer BIGGER. 6 ÷ ½ = 12, because there are twelve halves in 6. Dividing by a fraction greater than 1 makes it smaller.

**Complex fractions:** (1/2)/(3/4) is just ½ ÷ ¾ = ½ × 4/3 = 2/3. And a fraction over a sum, like 1/(1/2 + 1/3), means first work out the bottom: 5/6, then flip: 6/5.

> [!TRAP]
> 1/(a + b) is not 1/a + 1/b. You can only split a fraction when the SUM is on top: (a + b)/c = a/c + b/c, but c/(a + b) cannot be split.

## Comparing fractions

Three fast methods, in order of preference:

1. **Same denominator or same numerator.** With the same bottom, the bigger top wins. With the same top, the smaller bottom wins (3/5 > 3/7, because fifths are bigger than sevenths).
2. **Cross-multiply.** To compare a/b and c/d (positive numbers), compare a × d with c × b. For 5/8 vs 7/11: 5 × 11 = 55 and 7 × 8 = 56, so 7/11 is slightly bigger.
3. **Benchmark against ½ or 1.** 5/11 is just under ½ (since 5.5 would be half), and 6/11 is just over. How far a fraction is from 1 tells you its size: 7/8 is 1/8 short of 1, and 9/10 is 1/10 short, so 9/10 is bigger.

Careful with negative fractions: −1/2 is bigger than −2/3 (closer to zero).

### Worked example 1 (Easy)
Which is the greatest: 5/9, 7/12, 11/20, 3/5, 4/7?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

Convert each to a decimal (or compare against ⅗ = 0.6): 5/9 ≈ 0.556, 7/12 ≈ 0.583, 11/20 = 0.55, 3/5 = 0.6, 4/7 ≈ 0.571.

The greatest is 3/5.

Faster way: all of the others are noticeably under 0.6. 5/9 < 3/5 because 25 < 27; 7/12 < 3/5 because 35 < 36; 11/20 < 3/5 because 55 < 60; 4/7 < 3/5 because 20 < 21 (cross-multiplying each against 3/5).

Trap: choosing the fraction with the biggest numbers, such as 11/20. Size of the digits doesn't matter, only the ratio.

</details>

## Terminating and repeating decimals

When you turn a fraction into a decimal, one of two things happens: it stops (terminating) or it repeats forever.

**The rule:** reduce the fraction to lowest terms first. If the denominator's only prime factors are 2 and/or 5, the decimal terminates. If any other prime (3, 7, 11…) is left in the denominator, it repeats.

- 7/40: 40 = 2³ × 5, so it terminates (0.175).
- 5/12 reduces to 5/12: 12 = 2² × 3, the 3 causes a repeat (0.41666…).
- 9/12 reduces to 3/4, so it terminates (0.75). The raw 12 fooled you; always reduce first.

> [!TRAP]
> Check whether the fraction reduces before you judge by the denominator. 21/35 = 3/5 terminates, even though 35 has a 7.

How many decimal places does a terminating decimal have? The larger of the exponents of 2 and 5 in the reduced denominator. 3/40 has 40 = 2³ × 5¹, so 3 decimal places (0.075). Reason: you need to multiply the bottom up to 10³ = 1,000 to clear it.

**Repeating patterns.** 1/7 = 0.142857 142857… repeats in blocks of six digits. To find the 50th digit, find the position in the block: 50 ÷ 6 leaves remainder 2, so it's the 2nd digit of the block: 4. For repeating-block questions, always find the block length first, then use the remainder.

**Repeating decimal to fraction:** 0.333… = 1/3, 0.272727… = 27/99 = 3/11, 0.0555… = 5/90 = 1/18. For a block of n digits, put the block over n nines.

## Fractions of a total, and "fraction of the remainder"

This is where the GMAT turns fractions into word problems. The critical question is always: **a fraction of what?**

Suppose Priya has ₹1,200. She spends ⅓ on rent, then ½ **of the remainder** on groceries. Rent is 400, the remainder is 800, groceries are 400, and she has 400 left.

Notice what happens if the problem said "½ of the *original*": groceries would be 600, and she'd have 200 left. Same words, different base, different answer.

**The multiplication method (the fastest way):** if a fraction is left over after each step, multiply the leftovers. After spending ⅓ she has ⅔ left; after spending ½ of that, she has ½ of ⅔ left = ⅓ of the original. Always track what's *left* (or what's used) as a fraction of the original.

Always ask the question "of what?" before you multiply.

> [!TIP]
> When the total isn't given, pick a convenient number: the LCM of all the denominators. If thirds and quarters appear, assume the total is 12 (or 24). The answer fractions don't depend on the number you choose, so you're free to pick.

> [!TRAP]
> "⅓ spent, then ¼ spent" does not leave 1 − ⅓ − ¼ unless both fractions are of the original. If the second one is "of the remainder", the amount left is ⅔ × ¾ = ½.

### Worked example 2 (Medium)
A library lends out ⅖ of its books on Monday. On Tuesday it lends out ¼ of the books that were still on the shelf. What fraction of the original books is on the shelf after Tuesday?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

After Monday: 1 − ⅖ = ⅗ remain.

Tuesday lends ¼ of those, so ¾ of the ⅗ remain: ¾ × ⅗ = 9/20.

Check with a number: say 100 books. Monday: 40 lent, 60 left. Tuesday: 15 lent (¼ of 60), 45 left. 45/100 = 9/20. ✓.

Faster way: multiply the "stays on shelf" fractions straight through: ⅗ × ¾.

Trap: subtracting ⅖ + ¼ = 13/20 and answering 7/20. The ¼ is of the remainder, not of the original.

</details>

### Worked example 3 (Medium)
Which of the following fractions, when written as a decimal, will terminate? 

A. 7/15 &nbsp; B. 13/60 &nbsp; C. 21/84 &nbsp; D. 11/18 &nbsp; E. 9/22

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

Reduce each and look at the prime factors of the denominator.

- 7/15: denominator 3 × 5 has a 3, repeats.
- 13/60: 60 = 2² × 3 × 5, and 13 shares nothing with it, so the 3 stays; repeats.
- 21/84 = 1/4 (divide by 21). 4 = 2², so it terminates: 0.25.
- 11/18: 18 = 2 × 3², repeats.
- 9/22: 22 = 2 × 11, repeats.

The answer is C. 

Trap: judging 21/84 by the unreduced 84 = 2² × 3 × 7 and ruling it out. Always reduce first.

</details>

## Fraction tricks that save time

**Percent and fraction are interchangeable.** To find 37.5% of 160, think ⅜ of 160 = 60. To find 16⅔% of 450, think ⅙ × 450 = 75. Knowing the table lets you skip decimals.

**Add the same amount to top and bottom.** For a proper fraction (value below 1), adding the same positive number to numerator and denominator moves it *towards 1* (it gets bigger): 3/5 → 4/6 → 0.667 > 0.6.

**The "gap to 1" trick** for comparing: 7/8 (gap 1/8) versus 8/9 (gap 1/9). The smaller gap is the bigger fraction, so 8/9 > 7/8.

**Reciprocals flip order** for positive numbers: if x > y > 0, then 1/x < 1/y. So 1/3 < 1/2.

**Powers of fractions:** a number between 0 and 1 gets smaller when squared (¼)² = 1/16, and √(¼) = ½ is bigger than ¼. The GMAT often tests this.

**Split a sum on top:** (12 + 8x)/4 = 3 + 2x. This is allowed. But 4/(12 + 8x) cannot be split.

**Sum of unit fractions** (work problems, rates): 1/a + 1/b = (a + b)/(ab). If A takes 6 hours and B takes 3 hours, together they do 1/6 + 1/3 = ½ per hour, so 2 hours.

### Worked example 4 (Hard)
In a certain school, ⅔ of the students are girls. ¾ of the girls and ⅖ of the boys take the optional art class. If 57 students take art, how many students attend the school?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

Let the number of students be N. Girls = ⅔N, boys = ⅓N.

Art girls = ¾ × ⅔N = ½N. Art boys = ⅖ × ⅓N = 2/15 N.

Total in art = ½N + 2/15 N = 15/30 N + 4/30 N = 19/30 N.

So 19/30 N = 57, which gives N = 57 × 30/19 = 3 × 30 = 90.

Check: girls 60, boys 30. Art girls 45, art boys 12. 45 + 12 = 57 ✓.

Faster way: assume 30 students (20 girls, 10 boys: 15 girls and 4 boys take art, so 19 of 30) and then scale. 57 ÷ 19 = 3, so the school is 3 × 30 = 90.

Trap: adding ¾ + ⅖ as if they were fractions of the same group. They apply to different groups (girls and boys), so each must be applied to its own group size first.

</details>

1. Compute 2/3 + 3/4 − 1/6.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Common denominator 12: 8/12 + 9/12 − 2/12 = 15/12 = 5/4 = 1¼.</details>

2. What is (3/8) ÷ (9/16)?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>3/8 × 16/9 = 48/72 = 2/3.</details>

3. Convert 0.375 to a fraction and 7/8 to a percent.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>0.375 = 3/8. 7/8 = 87.5%.</details>

4. Does 17/80 terminate? How many decimal places?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>80 = 2⁴ × 5, only 2s and 5s, so yes. The larger exponent is 4, so 4 decimal places (0.2125).</details>

5. A bag of rice: ¼ is used for lunch, then ⅓ of what remains is used for dinner. What fraction of the bag is left?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>¾ × ⅔ = ½.</details>

## Key takeaways
- Add or subtract only after finding a common denominator; never add denominators.
- Cancel before you multiply. Dividing by a fraction means multiplying by its reciprocal.
- Compare fractions by cross-multiplying, or against benchmarks like ½ and 1.
- Memorise the fraction-decimal-percent table for halves through twelfths and the eighths.
- Reduce first, then check the denominator: only 2s and 5s means the decimal terminates.
- "Fraction of the remainder" means multiply what's left; "fraction of the original" means subtract from the whole. Always ask "of what?".
- When no total is given, pick a convenient one (the LCM of the denominators).
- You may split (a + b)/c, but never c/(a + b).
