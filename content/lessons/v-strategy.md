# Verbal Section Strategy

The Verbal section is 23 questions in 45 minutes. You don't win it with speed alone, and you don't win it with careful reading alone. You win it by spending time where it pays and by building the stamina to stay sharp for the full 45 minutes.

## The shape of the section

- **23 questions, 45 minutes**, so the average is about **1 minute 57 seconds** per question.
- A mix of **Critical Reasoning (CR)** and **Reading Comprehension (RC)**. There's no Sentence Correction in the Focus Edition.
- RC comes in passages (roughly 200 to 350 words) with 3 to 4 questions each. CR questions are standalone, each with a short stimulus.
- Questions are shown one at a time. You can bookmark and later use Review & Edit to change up to three answers per section.

Because CR and RC are mixed, you can't plan "CR first, then RC". You'll see them in an order driven by the adaptive algorithm, so you need to be flexible.

## Pacing targets

| Question type | Target time | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Critical Reasoning | about 2 minutes (90 to 120 seconds) | Read the stem first, then the stimulus, then predict |
| RC passage, including reading | 6 to 8 minutes per passage of 3 to 4 questions | About 1.5 to 2.5 minutes reading, then 1 to 1.5 minutes per question |
| Single RC question after the passage is read | 60 to 90 seconds | The passage should already be in your head |

If an RC passage with four questions takes you 7 minutes, that's 1:45 per question, which beats the average. This is why RC is where you can bank time for harder CR questions.

> [!TIP]
> Check your pace at three points: after question 8 you should have used about 15 minutes; after question 16, about 31 minutes; after question 23, 45 minutes (ideally with a minute to spare for review). If you're more than two minutes behind at any checkpoint, speed up on the next easy-looking question. Don't rush hard ones.

## How adaptive scoring works in Verbal

The test chooses each question based on how you answered the ones before it. Get questions right and the next ones get harder; get them wrong and they get easier. Your score depends on both how many you answer correctly and how hard those questions were.

Three consequences matter for you:

1. **The first handful of questions matter, but don't panic.** The algorithm adjusts quickly. One early mistake won't ruin the section.
2. **Hard questions feeling hard is a good sign.** If the questions feel difficult, you're probably doing well. Don't read difficulty as a sign you're failing.
3. **Never leave questions blank.** Unanswered questions are penalised, so a guess is always better than a blank.

> [!NOTE]
> Don't try to work out the algorithm during the test. You cannot know which questions are "experimental" or how hard each one is. Treat every question the same: do your best and move on.

## Eliminating with confidence

On GMAT Verbal, you rarely find the right answer by spotting it. You find it by killing the four wrong ones. Wrong answers are usually wrong in a specific, nameable way:

- **Out of scope:** talks about something the passage or stimulus doesn't cover.
- **Opposite:** says the reverse of what the text supports.
- **Too strong or extreme:** "always", "never", "all", "completely", "only".
- **Half right:** starts correctly and then adds something unsupported.
- **True but irrelevant:** a fact from the passage that doesn't answer this question.
- **Wrong comparison:** compares two things the text does not compare.

When you cross out a choice, name its fault in one word (scope, opposite, extreme, half, irrelevant). If you can't name a fault, don't cross it out yet.

> [!TRAP]
> A choice is not right because it sounds true, sensible, or intelligent. It's right only if the text supports it, or (in CR) if it does the specific job the question asks for. Many wrong answers are things a reasonable person would agree with.

## The "two finalists" decision process

Most hard questions come down to two choices. Here's a routine for choosing:

1. **Re-read the question stem.** Many errors come from answering a different question (weaken instead of strengthen, for example).
2. **Look for the one word that differs.** The two finalists are usually close. Find the specific word or phrase where they differ ("some" versus "most", "caused" versus "contributed to").
3. **Test that word against the text.** Does the passage or stimulus support the stronger or more specific word? If not, choose the more modest finalist.
4. **For CR, apply the question's test.** For Weaken: if this is true, does the conclusion become less likely? For Assumption: negate it, and does the argument collapse? For Inference: must it be true?
5. **Decide and move on.** If after 30 seconds you still can't split them, pick the one with fewer assumptions and mark it for review.

> [!REMEMBER]
> In inference and RC questions, the correct answer is usually the more cautious one. In weaken, strengthen and assumption questions, it's the one that most directly affects the gap in the argument.

### Worked example 3 (Hard): two finalists

A CR inference question has two finalists: (C) "Most of the town's jobs are in manufacturing" and (D) "At least some of the town's jobs are in manufacturing." The stimulus says: "Manufacturing is the largest source of jobs in the town." Which should you choose?

<details><summary>Show solution</summary>

The word that differs is "most" versus "at least some". "Largest source of jobs" could be 25 percent of jobs, well short of "most". So (C) goes beyond what the stimulus supports. (D) must be true if the largest source of jobs provides any jobs at all. Choose (D).

Trap: (C). "Largest" gets mistaken for "majority".

</details>

## Reading stamina

Verbal rewards a reader who can stay focused for 45 minutes. Stamina is a skill, and you build it by training.

- **Practise in blocks of 45 minutes** with no phone. Do this at least weekly, building up to twice a week in the final two months.
- **Read passages actively.** After each paragraph, ask yourself: "What was the point of that paragraph?" This stops your eyes moving while your brain wanders.
- **Take the optional 10-minute break wisely.** If you take it, walk, drink water, and eat something small. Don't check your phone.
- **Protect your sleep.** Reading comprehension suffers more from tiredness than quant does.

## Building a daily reading habit

The GMAT's passages are written in a particular style: formal, balanced, and abstract. If your daily diet is short social posts, a 400-word passage on soil ecology will feel like a wall. The cure is 20 to 30 minutes a day of the right kind of reading.

**What to read:**

- *The Economist*, especially the Science and Technology, Business, and Finance sections. It's the closest everyday match to the GMAT's tone.
- *Aeon* (aeon.co) and *Psyche*, for long, argued essays on science, history, and ideas.
- *Scientific American* and *Nautilus*, for science passages with a clear thesis.
- *The Atlantic*, *Harvard Business Review*, and *MIT Technology Review* for business and technology.
- *Mint*, *The Hindu* Op-Ed page and *Frontline* for strong Indian opinion writing with careful structure.
- The *Financial Times* "Big Read" or *The Guardian* long reads, when you want a long article.

**How to read them for GMAT:**

1. Pick one article a day, around 800 to 1,200 words.
2. Before reading, spend 10 seconds predicting what the article will argue.
3. After each paragraph, jot one phrase: its job (introduces problem, gives evidence, raises objection).
4. When you finish, write the author's main point in one sentence and the author's tone in one word (critical, neutral, enthusiastic).
5. Underline any sentence that starts with "However", "Yet", or "Although". These are the pivots the GMAT likes to test.
6. Once a week, find an argument in an op-ed and ask: what's the conclusion, what are the premises, and what gap would a critic attack?

> [!TIP]
> Keep a small "register notebook" for words and phrases that appear in GMAT-style writing but that you rarely use: "contend", "posit", "attribute to", "by contrast", "albeit", "notwithstanding", "to the extent that". Aim for 5 new ones a week and use each in a sentence of your own.

## Sharpening the "GMAT register" of English

Many Indian learners are fluent speakers and writers, but the GMAT uses a formal, precise register that differs from everyday English, and from Indian business English too. A few habits help:

- **Notice hedging.** GMAT authors say "may", "tends to", "suggests", "is likely to", "in many cases". These small words decide whether an answer is supported. Practise reading "suggests" as weaker than "shows" and "shows" as weaker than "proves".
- **Know the logic words.** "Whereas", "albeit", "notwithstanding", "insofar as", "hence", "thereby" and "moreover" each signal a specific logical relationship. Learn them as tools, not decoration.
- **Watch quantifiers.** "Some", "many", "most", "all", "few", "a few", "hardly any". The difference between "few" (almost none) and "a few" (some) can flip an answer.
- **Beware familiar phrases with different meanings.** Words like "mitigate" (soften), "tentative" (not final), "ambivalent" (mixed feelings), and "contend" (argue) appear often. Keep a short list of those you misread.
- **Read aloud sometimes.** Reading a dense paragraph aloud slows you down enough to catch its logic.
- **Don't translate.** If you catch yourself translating an English sentence into another language in your head to understand it, practise reading short English summaries first and then expand.

## Putting it together: a typical Verbal plan

| Phase | Weeks | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation | 1 to 4 | Learn the CR types and the RC method untimed; start the daily reading habit |
| Accuracy | 5 to 10 | Timed sets of 5 to 10 questions, review every wrong answer by naming the fault |
| Speed and stamina | 11 to 16 | Full 23-question sections in 45 minutes, twice a week |
| Polish | final 3 weeks | Mixed practice; review only error log; protect sleep |

## Key takeaways

- Verbal gives you about 1:57 per question. Aim for about 2 minutes on CR and 6 to 8 minutes per RC passage, reading included.
- Check pace at questions 8 and 16, and don't spend more than about 2.5 minutes on any one question.
- Adaptive scoring means hard questions are a good sign. Never leave a question blank.
- Eliminate by naming each wrong choice's fault: scope, opposite, extreme, half-right, irrelevant, wrong comparison.
- With two finalists, re-read the stem, find the single differing word, and test it against the text.
- Build stamina with 45-minute blocks and active reading, not passive scanning.
- Read 20 to 30 minutes a day from sources like The Economist, Aeon, and Scientific American, and note the logic of each argument.
- Learn the GMAT register: hedges, quantifiers and logic words.
