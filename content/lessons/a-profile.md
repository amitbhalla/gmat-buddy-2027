# Building Your Profile as a Marketing Professional

Admissions committees don't want perfect people. They want people who've already started leading, who can show results, and who have a reason for the next step. You have about ten months before you start writing applications, and that's enough time to change what your profile says about you.

> [!NOTE]
> We found no school guidance specific to marketing profiles. What follows combines what schools say publicly (holistic review, leadership, clear goals) with sound admissions practice. Treat it as advice, not a rulebook.

## What schools say they look for

ISB describes its process as holistic and invites you to showcase achievements outside work. London Business School's essays ask about your motivations, evidence of leadership and teamwork, why this degree, and what you'd contribute to the community. Imperial asks for a well-researched career plan with clear short- and long-term goals. Chicago Booth mentions leadership, collaboration and respect for others.

Put simply, they want four things:

1. **Impact:** what changed because of you.
2. **Leadership:** you moved others, with or without a title.
3. **Direction:** you know why this degree and why now.
4. **Fit and potential:** you can handle the work and you'd add something to the class.

## Impact: turn your job into numbers

Marketing is full of metrics, which is an advantage. The trap is listing activity instead of results.

| Weak | Stronger |
|---|---|
| "Managed social media" | "Grew Instagram following from 8,000 to 21,000 in six months, lifting qualified leads 35%" |
| "Ran email campaigns" | "Redesigned the welcome email series, raising open rates from 22% to 34%" |
| "Helped with events" | "Coordinated a 300-person product launch on a budget of INR 6 lakh, 12% under plan" |

If your numbers aren't impressive, the scale and your own contribution still matter. Always say what you did, not what the team did.

> [!TIP]
> Start a "wins file" today. One document, one line per win, with the date, the number and who noticed. In a year you'll have thirty entries and your resume and essays will almost write themselves.

## Leadership without a title

With 1 to 3 years of experience, you won't have a team of ten. That's normal. Admissions readers look for evidence of leading in small ways:

- You led a project, even a small one.
- You trained or mentored an intern or a new joiner.
- You took initiative nobody asked for: a new process, a new report, a new channel.
- You persuaded a sceptical senior person.
- You organised something outside work, like a college alumni event or a community drive.

Aim to pick one project in the next few months where you can be the clear owner. Ask your manager for it directly. A good phrase: "Could I own the launch of X from start to finish, and report back on the result?"

## Beyond the day job

Extracurriculars matter more for early-career applicants than for older ones, because there's less work history to lean on. But quality beats quantity. One sustained, meaningful commitment is better than six one-off events.

Examples that work well:

- Teaching or mentoring (English classes, career guidance for school students).
- Volunteering with an organisation for more than a year.
- Alumni leadership, such as Jesus and Mary College or DU societies.
- Sports, arts or writing that you've kept up and can speak about with energy.
- Community projects you started yourself.

> [!TRAP]
> Don't invent activities to fill a list. Interviewers ask about them in detail. If you can't tell a vivid two-minute story about it, drop it.

## Certifications and quant readiness

Schools worry a little about whether humanities and commerce graduates can handle quant-heavy coursework. You can answer that worry before it's asked.

| Signal | Why it helps |
|---|---|
| Strong GMAT Quant section | The most direct proof |
| A course in statistics, data analysis or accounting | Shows you've done it, not just claimed it |
| Marketing analytics tools (Google Analytics, Meta Blueprint, HubSpot Academy) | Practical, quick, and relevant to your role |
| Using data in your work (A/B tests, dashboards, budgets) | Real-world evidence |

On certifications: free and low-cost ones from Google, HubSpot and Meta are fine to include, but they're a small signal. Think of them as supporting evidence, not the headline. A completed analytics project at work is worth more than three certificates.

For quant readiness, choose one course: a university-affiliated statistics or accounting course online, with a certificate you can list. Finish it before your applications go in.

## The story: why management, why now

Every application needs a through-line. A weak story says "I want to grow and learn." A strong one says:

1. Here's what I've done (evidence).
2. Here's the ceiling I've hit (the gap).
3. Here's what this programme gives me that I can't get at work.
4. Here's what I'll do with it (a short-term role and a long-term vision).

For Maria, a draft might sound like this: "In two years of marketing, I've learned I'm good at understanding customers, but I don't yet understand how pricing, operations and finance decisions are made. I want to move from running campaigns to shaping brand and growth strategy."

That's not final, but it has tension and direction. We'll work on it in the essays lesson.

## A ten-month profile plan

| When | Action |
|---|---|
| Nov 2026 to Jan 2027 | Start the wins file; pick one project to own |
| Feb to Apr 2027 | Run the project; collect numbers; ask for feedback from your manager |
| May to Jul 2027 | Finish a quant or analytics course; formalise volunteering hours |
| Aug 2027 | Update the wins file; draft your "why now" paragraph |
| Jun to Aug 2027 | Choose recommenders; share your wins file when you brief them |

## Worked example: turning a bullet into a profile story

A typical bullet: "Worked on a loyalty campaign for retail customers."

<details><summary>Show the rewrite</summary>

Ask four questions: What did you own? How big was it? What changed? Who noticed?

Answers: She wrote the campaign brief and coordinated the agency. It reached 40,000 customers. Repeat purchases rose 18% over three months. Her director asked her to present the results to the leadership team.

Rewrite: "Led a loyalty campaign for 40,000 retail customers, writing the brief and managing the agency; repeat purchases rose 18% in three months, and I presented results to the leadership team."

Leadership evidence: briefing and coordinating an agency, presenting to senior leaders.

Story seed: she enjoyed seeing data change a decision and wants to learn how to shape the next decision, not just report on the last one.

Trap: adding numbers you can't defend. Keep only figures you can explain in an interview.

## Quick check
1. What's the difference between activity and impact?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Activity is what you did ("managed social media"). Impact is what changed because of it ("grew leads 35%").</details>

2. Name two ways to show leadership without a team.
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Owning a project end to end, mentoring a junior colleague, or starting something nobody asked for.</details>

3. Why might a humanities or commerce graduate need to show "quant readiness"?
<details><summary>Answer</summary>Programmes are quant-heavy, and showing evidence of numeracy answers the question before it's asked.</details>

## Key takeaways
- Schools look for impact, leadership, direction and fit, not perfection.
- Turn work activity into quantified results, and be clear about your own part.
- Leadership counts without a title: ownership, mentoring and initiative.
- One sustained extracurricular beats a long list.
- Use the GMAT Quant section plus one course or work project to show quant readiness.
- Build a "why management, why now" story with evidence, a gap and a plan.
