## A letter before we start

You're about to prepare for an exam that most people find stressful, and you're going to do it while holding down a full-time marketing job. That's a real thing, and plenty of people have done it well. The ones who do tend to share one habit: they show up in small, boring, regular doses instead of waiting for a heroic weekend.

So here's my promise. I won't pretend this is easy, and I won't bury you in panic. Every day the app will tell you what to do, and your only job is to do that and then stop. You don't need to decide what to study. You need to arrive.

A quick word on the maths. You haven't done serious maths since school, and that's completely fine. The current GMAT is built for people like you: no geometry, no sentence-grammar rules, no essay. Quant is arithmetic and algebra only. Your marketing job has probably given you more data-reading practice than you realise, and that helps with a whole section of the exam.

## What the app does for you

Think of the app as a coach who never gets tired. Here are its parts, and how each one fits in.

**Today.** This is your home screen. Each morning it shows a short plan for the day: a lesson to read, a set of practice questions, a flashcard review, maybe an error-log session. If you only open one screen, open this one. It changes as you improve, so it's never stale.

**Lessons.** Short, plain-English teaching notes. Quant lessons start from zero. Verbal and Data Insights lessons teach you how the question writers think. Every lesson ends with key takeaways, so you can revise in two minutes later.

**Practice.** Original questions in the style of the real exam, grouped by topic and difficulty. Early on you'll do these untimed. Later they get timed. Every question has a full explanation, a tip, and the trap that catches people.

**Error log.** Every time you get something wrong, the app asks you to record why. It sounds like paperwork, but it's the single most useful habit in the whole plan. We'll cover it in detail in the study-system lesson.

**Flashcards.** Formulas, rules and traps come back to you at spaced intervals, so they stay in your head without hours of re-reading.

**Mocks.** Full-length, timed practice exams that copy the real thing. You'll take them from the middle of the journey onward.

**Progress.** Your accuracy by topic, your pace, your score trend. It tells you honestly where you stand, which is better than a feeling.

**Admissions.** The other half of this project. The GMAT is only the key to the door. This section helps you plan ISB and European Master's applications, so the exam and the applications are planned together.

> [!TIP]
> The app is built for your phone. Ten minutes in a Metro ride is worth doing. Don't wait for a perfect two-hour block that never arrives.

## The ten-month journey

Today is 20 October 2026. Your plan starts tomorrow, 21 October, and your first attempt is on Saturday 28 August 2027. That's roughly 44 weeks. Here's how we use them, in five phases.

| Phase | Roughly when | What you're doing |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Foundations | late Oct to mid-Dec 2026 | Rebuild school maths, learn the exam format, take a first diagnostic, set up your error log |
| 2. Core | mid-Dec 2026 to Mar 2027 | Work through every Quant, Verbal and Data Insights topic, mostly untimed |
| 3. Advanced | Apr to May 2027 | Harder questions, mixed sets, start timing yourself, fix weak spots |
| 4. Mock season | Jun to mid-Aug 2027 | Full mocks, careful reviews, pacing and stamina |
| 5. Test & Apply | late Aug 2027 onward | Exam day, score decisions, applications |

Those dates are guides, not walls. If a phase needs two more weeks, we take them. The plan is built with slack so that a bad month at work doesn't wreck everything.

### Phase 1: Foundations

You'll do a first diagnostic in the opening weeks. It will probably feel rough. That's the point: it gives us a baseline, not a verdict. After that you'll rebuild the basics (fractions, percents, ratios, algebra) and learn how the exam is structured.

### Phase 2: Core

This is the longest and most important stretch. You'll move through each topic: learn it, practise it untimed, log your errors, and review them. Quant, Verbal and Data Insights run side by side so you don't forget one while focusing on another.

### Phase 3: Advanced

Now the questions get harder and mixed together, as they will be in the real exam. You'll start using a timer, and you'll learn when to give up on a question (yes, that's a skill).

### Phase 4: Mock season

Full-length mocks, one every couple of weeks, with a long review after each. You'll test your section order, your break plan and your nerves. We'll talk about mocks properly in their own lesson.

### Phase 5: Test & Apply

Exam day on 28 August, then the score decision, then the applications. There's also a backup attempt pencilled in for mid-October 2027. I'll explain why in a moment.

> [!NOTE]
> Scores from your GMAT attempts can now be combined. GMAC launched a feature called the GMAT Superscore in August 2026, which takes your best section scores across valid attempts. That makes a planned second attempt a smart safety net, not a sign of failure. We'll go through it in the scores and retakes lesson.

## A realistic weekly rhythm

Let's be honest about hours. The plan assumes about 8 to 10 hours a week. Over 44 weeks that's somewhere around 350 to 440 hours, which is a solid amount for a first-time preparation. Prep companies often suggest about 180 hours for a gain of 50 to 80 points and 240 to 360 hours for a jump of 100 to 150 points, though these are vendor estimates and no one publishes an exact formula for you personally. The aim isn't to hit a number. It's to be consistent.

Here's one way a working week can look.

| Day | What | Time |
|---|---|---|
| Monday to Friday | Today plan on your phone: one lesson or practice set, plus flashcards | 40 to 60 min each day |
| Saturday | Longer session: a topic block, a timed set, or a mock review | 2.5 to 3 hours |
| Sunday | Error-log review, flashcards, a look at next week | 1 to 1.5 hours |

That adds up to roughly 8 to 10 hours. Notice there's no seventh heroic day. One day a week should have no GMAT in it at all. Rest is part of the plan, because tired brains don't hold on to anything.

Some weeks will go wrong. A deadline lands, a family function takes your weekend, you get a cold. When that happens, do the minimum: flashcards and a ten-minute practice set keep the habit alive. Then get back to the plan. Missing a week doesn't undo ten months of work, but quietly dropping the habit does.

> [!TIP]
> Pick fixed slots and attach them to something you already do. For example: flashcards on the Metro, one practice set after dinner, the long session on Saturday morning before the day fills up.

## How I'll talk to you

I'll be direct. If a method is a trap, I'll say so. If something is unproven, I'll say it's unproven. When I'm quoting a fact about the exam that comes from a prep company rather than the exam board, I'll tell you, and I'll point you to mba.com to confirm. The GMAT rules and prices can change, so the official site is always the final word.

I won't promise a particular score. Nobody honestly can. What I can promise is that if you follow the plan, review your mistakes properly and take the mocks seriously, you'll give yourself a real, competitive shot at the 655 to 705 and above range that schools like ISB and European programmes tend to see.

## Your first-week checklist

You don't have to do all this on day one. Spread it across your first seven days.

1. **Read the next two strategy lessons.** "The GMAT Explained" and "Scoring" will make the whole exam feel familiar before you start.
2. **Create your mba.com account** at the official GMAT site. It's free, and it's where you'll later book the exam, get the free Starter Kit and see your scores. Use your name exactly as it appears on your passport.
3. **Download the free GMAT Official Starter Kit** and note where Practice Exams 1 and 2 are. Don't take Exam 1 yet. We'll use it as a proper diagnostic once you've seen the format.
4. **Block your study slots in your calendar.** Weekday slots, one long Saturday block, one Sunday review. Treat them like meetings with a client.
5. **Open the app every day** and finish the Today plan, even if it takes 15 minutes.
6. **Start your error log** from your very first practice question. Habits are easier to build on day one than on day sixty.
7. **Tell one person your plan.** A friend, a sibling, a colleague. Not for pressure, just so the goal exists outside your head.
8. **Write down why you're doing this.** One sentence about the MBA or Master's you want and what it opens up. Put it somewhere you'll see it. You'll need it around month five.

> [!REMEMBER]
> The goal this week is not to learn much. It's to set up the machine: account, calendar, habits. The learning gets easier once the routine is running.

## Key takeaways

- Consistency beats intensity: small daily sessions plus one long weekend block is the whole secret.
- The app runs on a daily Today plan. Open it, do it, stop.
- The journey has five phases: Foundations, Core, Advanced, Mock season, then Test & Apply.
- Plan for 8 to 10 hours a week, with at least one full rest day.
- Start the error log from your first question. It's the habit that pays back the most.
- The GMAT has no geometry, no sentence-grammar section and no essay, so a rusty maths background isn't a disadvantage you can't fix.
- Always confirm prices, rules and policies on mba.com before acting on them. They can change.
