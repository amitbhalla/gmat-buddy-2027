# Scoring, Percentiles and Your Target

"What score do I need?" is the question everyone asks first and understands last. This lesson explains how the scoring works, what a score means compared with other people, and how to set a target that fits the schools you're aiming for.

## The scale

Every section is scored from **60 to 90**. Your total score runs from **205 to 805**, in steps of 10. The three sections (Quant, Verbal, Data Insights) are weighted equally, so each one is worth a third of your total.

![Infographic titled How Your GMAT Score Works. Cards Quant 60–90, Verbal 60–90 and Data Insights 60–90 are added together into a Total 205–805 card. Caption: All three sections count equally. Scale markers: 555 ≈ 47th percentile, 655 ≈ 90th percentile, 705 ≈ 98th percentile. Footnote: Percentiles are approximate.](img/gen/score-scale.jpg)


GMAC doesn't publish the exact formula for turning section scores into a total. Prep companies estimate that it works roughly like this: add the three section scores, subtract 180, multiply by about 6.67 and add 205. Treat that as an approximation, not a rule. It does show you something useful: one extra point in any single section moves your total by about 6 to 7 points.

| Section scores (Q / V / DI) | Rough total |
|---|---|
| 75 / 75 / 75 | about 505 to 515 |
| 80 / 80 / 80 | about 605 |
| 82 / 83 / 84 | about 655 to 665 |
| 85 / 85 / 85 | about 705 |

These are illustrative only. The real conversion on your score report is final.

> [!NOTE]
> Because sections are equal, a weak section drags the total just as much as a strong one lifts it. A balanced profile is a good aim. You don't have to be brilliant at everything, but you can't afford a disaster in one section.

## Percentiles: what a score means

A percentile tells you what share of test-takers you beat. A 655 at the 90th percentile means you scored higher than about 90 percent of recent test-takers.

Percentiles are refreshed every year by GMAC, and I couldn't read the official 2026 table directly. The figures below come from a coaching site that attributes them to GMAC data for 2021 to 2026, so please treat them as approximate and check the current table on mba.com.

| Total score | Approx. percentile |
|---|---|
| 735 | 99 |
| 705 | 98 |
| 685 | 95 |
| 675 | 94 |
| 665 | 91 |
| 655 | 90 |
| 645 | 86 |
| 625 | 78 |
| 605 | 69 |
| 555 | 47 |

Notice how it flattens at the top. Going from 555 to 605 takes you past 22 percent of test-takers. Going from 685 to 735 only gains you four percentage points. That matters for your goals: past a certain point, extra points give schools less new information about you.

Sections have their own percentiles too. One coaching site estimates that a Quant score of 78 is around the 50th percentile, a Verbal 78 is nearer the 37th, and a Data Insights 78 is around the 69th. The averages differ by section, which is why you shouldn't compare section scores across sections by eye. Again, these are approximate.

## What score do you need?

There's no single answer, but here's how to think about it.

**ISB.** ISB's published class profile for the class of 2026 shows a GMAT mean of 683, with a middle-80-percent range of 635 to 730. ISB's page doesn't say whether that mixes old-scale and Focus-scale scores, and a third-party site reports that the Focus average was lower than the old-scale one. So treat 683 as a mixed figure, not a pure Focus target. ISB also says there is no minimum score, and it looks at the whole profile: work experience, essays, academics and more.

**European Master's programmes.** Many MiM and marketing Master's programmes publish a lower range than MBAs, and some are flexible. You'll find specifics in the admissions section, and each school's site is the final word.

A sensible goal for you: **aim for 655 to 705 or above on the current scale.** That's roughly the 90th to 98th percentile, and it sits comfortably within what competitive programmes see. If you land at 645 or 655, you're not out of the game, especially with a strong profile. If you reach 685 or above, the GMAT stops being your weakest point.

> [!TIP]
> Set three numbers: a floor (the score you'd accept and apply with), a target, and a stretch. For example, 645, 675 and 705. It helps you make calm decisions after each mock.

## Old 800-scale scores and the concordance

You'll read forum posts, brochures and school pages that quote scores from the old exam (200 to 800). The two scales aren't the same. GMAC publishes a concordance table that links scores by percentile. The August 2026 table, as quoted in search results (I couldn't open the file myself), links an old 700 to a current 655, or sometimes 645, and an old 710 to a current 665. Other sources put the current 645 near an old 700 and a current 665 near an old 720.

Don't try to convert by feel. If a school quotes an average, check whether it's on the old or current scale, and use GMAC's concordance table on the official site for exact links.

> [!TRAP]
> "Everyone gets 700+" is an old-scale statement. A current 705 is the 98th percentile. Don't measure yourself against old-scale numbers you read in forums.

## Question-level adaptive testing, simply explained

The GMAT adapts to you question by question. Imagine a shop assistant offering you a shirt. If you say "Yes, nice", the next shirt costs more. If you say no, the next one costs less. The software is trying to find the level where you get about half right.

In practice:

- Your first few answers set the starting level for the rest of the section.
- Correct answers push later questions up in difficulty. Wrong answers push them down.
- Harder questions carry more weight, but only if you can answer them.
- You're not meant to feel good. If the questions feel hard near the end, it often means you're doing well.

> [!TIP]
> Don't judge your performance during the exam by how hard the questions feel. A hard run can mean you're scoring high. An easy run can mean the opposite. Just answer each question and move on.

## Why early questions matter

Prep companies explain that the adaptive engine learns a lot from your first handful of answers, and that early mistakes pull the difficulty level down in ways that are hard to recover from. GMAC doesn't publish how heavily each question counts, so exact numbers are guesses. The practical advice is still sound.

1. Treat roughly the first five to seven questions of every section as non-negotiable. Slow down slightly, read carefully, and avoid silly errors.
2. Don't over-invest, though. Spending five minutes on question 3 will damage questions 18 to 21 more than it helps.
3. Don't panic if you miss an early question. It's one answer among many.

And a caution: Review & Edit only lets you change up to three answers, and prep companies report that fixing an early mistake doesn't reset the difficulty path you've already been routed on (GMAC doesn't publish this). So the lesson is simply to get it right the first time.

## The unanswered-question penalty

Here's something many people don't know: **wrong answers aren't directly penalised, but unanswered ones are.** If time runs out and you've left questions blank, your section score goes down.

Prep companies estimate the penalty as 30 points on the 60 to 90 scale multiplied by the percentage of unanswered questions. By that estimate, one blank in a 23-question section is worth about 1.3 points. GMAC doesn't publish the exact formula, and I couldn't find official confirmation, but the advice doesn't change: **never leave a blank.** A guess has a 20 percent chance of being right; a blank has none.

We cover how to guess smartly in the pacing lesson.

## Standard error: why your score will wobble

Every test has measurement error. A coaching site reports that GMAC's standard error on the total score is roughly 30 to 40 points. I'd treat that figure as approximate, since it came from a secondary source. What it means is that if you took the exam twice on two different days with exactly the same ability, you could easily see differences of 20 or 30 points just from noise: how you slept, the specific questions you got, a lucky guess.

This affects how you read results.

- A mock score is a range, not a number. A 645 mock could be a 620 to 670 student.
- One bad exam doesn't mean you're worse than you thought.
- A 10-point difference between two mocks is usually meaningless. Look at the trend over three or four.

This is also why the Superscore is good news for you. It lets you keep the best section performances from different days, softening the effect of random bad luck.

## How to use the score in your planning

Your first diagnostic will probably land well below your target, and that's expected. Here's a rough guide to what to do with a score:

1. **Take your first official practice exam.** Note your total and three section scores.
2. **Find your weakest section** and your weakest question types within it.
3. **Compare to your target.** The gap tells you how much work is ahead. Prep companies suggest that a gap of 100 to 150 points takes roughly 240 to 360 hours of study, which fits well with your 10-month plan.
4. **Retest every few weeks** with a mock and track the trend.

## Key takeaways

- Section scores run 60 to 90, totals 205 to 805, and the three sections count equally.
- One section point is worth roughly 6 to 7 total points (a prep-company estimate, not an official formula).
- Percentiles flatten at the top: 655 is about the 90th percentile and 705 about the 98th (approximate).
- ISB's class-of-2026 mean was 683 (ISB doesn't say which scale). A target of 655 to 705 or above is sensible, with a personal floor, target and stretch.
- Old-scale and current-scale scores aren't the same. Use GMAC's concordance table.
- The exam adapts question by question, so the early questions deserve extra care.
- Never leave a question unanswered. Wrong answers aren't directly penalised, blanks are.
- Expect a standard error of roughly 30 to 40 points, so judge your progress by trends.
