# Recommendations: Choosing and Briefing Your Recommenders

A recommendation letter or evaluation is the one part of your application written by someone else. It tells the committee how you work when nobody's watching. A strong, specific recommendation can tip a close decision, and a vague one can quietly hurt you. The good news is that you can influence the quality, ethically, by choosing well and briefing well.

> [!NOTE]
> Requirements vary by school. From the research: ISB asks for one evaluation, from someone who has closely observed your recent work, preferably your current supervisor; family and friends aren't accepted. INSEAD requires two recommendations, with a third optional. Always check the current rules on the school's site.

## Who should recommend you

### The default: your direct supervisor

ISB says it prefers a current supervisor. That's the right instinct for most programmes. A supervisor sees your results, your judgment and your growth.

### When the supervisor isn't the best choice

Sometimes the person who knows your work best isn't your direct manager. Consider:

- A senior colleague who has led you on a major project.
- A previous manager, if you've recently changed teams.
- A client or senior partner, if your work involves close external relationships and the school allows it.

Choose a person who: knows your work in detail, thinks well of you, can write specific examples, and will meet the deadline.

> [!TRAP]
> Don't choose someone for their title. A glowing, detailed letter from a manager beats a three-line note from a CEO who barely knows you. Family, friends and academic acquaintances aren't accepted by ISB, so don't plan around them for that application.

## The awkward question: telling your manager

Many applicants worry about telling their boss they plan to leave for a degree. Here's a practical approach.

1. Don't surprise them. Pick a quiet moment and say you're planning to apply to business school in the next year.
2. Frame it around growth, not leaving. "I want to build my skills so I can take on bigger responsibilities."
3. Ask them if they'd feel comfortable supporting you. If not, it's better to learn that early.
4. Give them lots of notice, ideally two to three months before the deadline.

If you truly can't tell your supervisor, think carefully about the alternative. Some schools ask for a current supervisor and will notice if you can't offer one. If that's your situation, a short, honest explanation in the optional essay or the recommender relationship field can help.

## The recommender pack

Make it easy for your recommender to write a great letter. Send them a pack with:

| Item | Why |
|---|---|
| A one-page summary of your goals | So they know the story you're telling |
| Your resume | So they remember your timeline |
| Three to five specific examples you'd like them to consider | Prompts memory, avoids vague praise |
| The questions the school will ask | So they can prepare |
| The deadline and the submission instructions | Removes friction |
| A short note on what makes each school different | So they can tailor |
| A thank-you and a reminder date | Courtesy and structure |

> [!TIP]
> Ask them to meet or call for twenty minutes. Walking them through your goals brings the letter to life. Offer to draft a list of bullet-point reminders, but never write the letter for them. Schools can tell, and it's unethical.

### Example of an example

Instead of "Maria is a hard worker", the recommender should be able to write: "During our Diwali campaign, Maria noticed a 12% drop in email click-through, ran a quick A/B test on subject lines without being asked, and shared the finding with the team. Our click-through recovered within two weeks."

You can help by reminding them of the incident. You can't dictate their words.

## Typical questions you'll see

Most recommendation forms ask versions of the following. Understanding them helps you choose examples.

1. How long and in what capacity have you known the applicant?
2. What are the applicant's greatest strengths? Give examples.
3. What are the applicant's areas for development? Give examples.
4. Describe a time the applicant showed leadership.
5. How does the applicant compare with others at the same level?
6. Would you rehire or promote this person?
7. Is there anything else we should know?

The "areas for development" question makes applicants nervous. It's fine. An honest, constructive point (and how you responded to feedback) actually adds credibility. Agree beforehand with your recommender what to say, and make sure it's real.

## Timeline

Work backwards from your application deadline.

| When | Action |
|---|---|
| 3 to 4 months before | Quietly identify your recommenders |
| 10 to 12 weeks before | Have the first conversation |
| 8 weeks before | Send the recommender pack |
| 4 weeks before | Gentle reminder and offer to talk |
| 2 weeks before | Check they've received the portal link; confirm progress |
| 1 week before | Final reminder if needed |
| After submission | Thank them with a note |

For ISB Round 1 in about mid or late September 2027, that means starting conversations in June and July, and sending the pack in August. If you take the GMAT on 28 August 2027, brief your recommenders around that time so they aren't rushed.

## Waiving the right to read

Most online forms ask whether you'll waive your right to see the letter. Nearly all applicants waive it. Recommenders are more candid when they know you won't read it. Trust matters, so choose people you trust.

## If a recommender is late

Don't panic. Follow up politely, offer help with the link or format, and contact the admissions office if the deadline is at risk. Most schools have a process. Never write the letter or fill the form yourself.

## Worked example: choosing among three candidates

Maria has three possible recommenders.

- Priya: her direct manager of ten months. Warm, busy, writes carefully.
- Mr Khanna: the head of marketing. Senior, but they have worked closely only once.
- Neha: a senior colleague who led her on the launch project, but who isn't her manager.

For ISB, which prefers a supervisor who has closely observed recent work, Maria chooses Priya. She knows Maria's recent work and can give examples of growth.

Next, Maria schedules a twenty-minute chat with Priya in July, shares the pack in August, and sets a reminder for two weeks before the deadline.

## Key takeaways
- Choose the person who knows your work best, preferably your supervisor, not the most senior name.
- Tell your manager early and frame the plan around growth.
- Give recommenders a pack with goals, resume, examples, questions and deadlines.
- Never write or edit the letter yourself.
- Start the conversation about three months before the deadline.
- Waive your right to read the letter, and thank your recommenders afterward.
